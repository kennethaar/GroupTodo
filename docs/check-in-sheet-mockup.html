Waiting for updates

You are waiting on four things from Mia in Finance. Mia does not use GroupTodo and is not going to start today. So you send her one file, she ticks what she has done, and her answers come back into your list.

No account, no invite, no server — the same bargain as the rest of GroupTodo. The file is the round trip.

The exchange

Four steps, two of them yours

  1. Ask · you People → Mia Halvorsen → Ask for an update. The four things you are waiting on are already listed and already ticked to include. Press Send.
  2. Send · you GroupTodo writes check-in-mia-halvorsen.html — one self-contained file. Email it, Teams it, drop it in a share. Nothing is uploaded anywhere.
  3. Tick · Mia She double-clicks the file. It opens in whatever browser she has. She taps the two she finished, types one note, presses Send back — her email opens with the answers already in it, and she presses Send. Nothing to attach.
  4. Import · you Her answers land in your inbox as a few plain lines. Paste them into GroupTodo and you get this review: two tasks to close, one comment to file. Press Apply.

    Step 3b · the way back

    No attachment, on purpose

    The obvious design is a Send back that opens her email with the reply file already attached. That cannot be built, by anyone: a mailto: link carries to, subject, cc, bcc and body and nothing else. The non-standard attach= that one or two old desktop clients once honoured is ignored by every browser now — a web page that could pre-attach a file off your disk is a web page that could mail your disk to a stranger.

    So the reply does not travel as an attachment. It travels as the body of the email, which is better anyway: nothing to save, nothing to find again in Downloads, nothing to forget to attach. Mia presses one button and presses Send.

    Where that stops working. A mailto: is a URL, and Outlook truncates one around two thousand characters. The four tasks on this page, with two notes, make a link of 982 — comfortable, but not as comfortable as it looks, because a dozen tasks or one long paragraph would clear two thousand easily. So the sheet measures the encoded link every time she ticks or types: under budget the button opens her email, over budget it changes to Save the reply and attach it and says why. Quietly sending a truncated reply would be worse than either.
    What that budget bought. The machine-readable marker at the bottom of the reply was being pretty-printed, which spent four hundred characters — a fifth of the whole allowance — indenting something no human ever reads. On one line the same reply is 982 instead of 1,270. Worth knowing before this is built: in a design where the transport is a URL, whitespace is a budget line.

    And whatever route she takes — email body, attached file, pasted into Teams, read out over the phone and typed in by you — the import takes the same few lines. The text is the protocol. It is deliberately short enough that a person could retype it, because the one thing a round trip through other people's email cannot assume is that any particular route will work.

    The point

    The click count

    • 3 clicks for you to ask — person, Ask for an update, Send.
    • 1 click per thing Mia finished. Unfinished things cost her nothing.
    • 1 click for Mia to send it back.
    • 2 clicks for you to import — open the file, Apply.
    • 0 accounts created, on either side, ever.
